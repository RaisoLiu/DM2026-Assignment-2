\subsection{1(c) Observations}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small learning rates are robust to regularization strength.} Learning rates 0.005, 0.01, and 0.1 achieve similar CV accuracy ($\sim$72--73\%) across all $\lambda$ values, indicating that slow, stable convergence makes the model less sensitive to the regularization hyperparameter.
    \item \textbf{Large learning rate + strong regularization causes severe underfitting.} At lr=0.5, performance degrades sharply when $\lambda \geq 4.0$ (CV accuracy drops to 52.3\% and 46.6\%). When gradient steps are too large and regularization aggressively shrinks weights, the model cannot learn meaningful patterns.
    \item \textbf{Good generalization from CV to test.} The top-1 setting (lr=0.005, $\lambda$=4.0) achieves 73.1\% CV accuracy and 76.0\% test accuracy, and the top-2 setting generalizes similarly ($\sim$73\% $\to$ 74.7\%). This confirms that 5-fold CV on this dataset provides a reliable estimate of out-of-sample performance.
    \item The top-1 model shows higher recall (84.2\%) than precision (72.7\%), meaning it favors identifying positive samples at the cost of more false positives.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{C=100 balances fit and generalization:} The train--validation gap is moderate (2.25\%), and test performance is strong (97.0\% accuracy, 97.0\% F1). Although $C \in \{10, 1000, 10000\}$ achieve the same test accuracy, $C=100$ has the best validation score, making it the most reliable choice without peeking at test data.
\end{itemize}

This is not an apples-to-apples comparison, but it highlights that label information is critical for this classification task.
\end{itemize}

\section{Q5: Enhancing K-Means with Association Rule Mining}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Motivation and Inspiration}

Standard K-Means treats all features equally and cannot distinguish discriminative features from noise. We propose a \textbf{semi-supervised} enhancement that uses association rule mining to guide clustering. The approach explicitly leverages class labels during feature engineering, while K-Means itself remains unsupervised.

\textbf{Inspirations:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textit{TF-IDF-style feature importance}: We adapt the idea of term specificity (IDF) to use association rule lift as a proxy for discriminative power. Features that appear in high-lift rules for specific classes are analogous to terms with high IDF --- they carry more information.
    \item \textit{Supervised discretization} (Fayyad \& Irani, 1993): Class labels guide the mining process to identify feature patterns that maximize class separability, similar to how supervised binning optimizes split points.
    \item \textit{Feature augmentation for clustering}: Enriching the feature space with auxiliary pattern-based features before applying standard clustering, as discussed in lecture on pattern-based clustering.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Method Design}

We design two components and evaluate them both independently and jointly via ablation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Rule-Guided Feature Weighting (primary method, semi-supervised):} All 20 features are discretized (3:4:3 value range split) and association rules are mined \textit{per class} using FP-Growth (\texttt{min\_support=0.3}, confidence $\geq 0.5$, lift $\geq 1.0$). For each original feature, we accumulate the lift values from all rules in which the feature appears across all classes. The resulting importance scores are normalized to weights in $[1.0, 3.0]$ and applied to the standardized features. This step is explicitly label-guided.
    \item \textbf{Class-Stratified Pattern Features (experimental extension, semi-supervised):} The top 5 most discriminative patterns (ranked by lift $\times$ confidence) are selected from each class's rules, yielding 20 binary features indicating whether a sample matches each class-associated itemset.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/q5_feature_weights.png}
\caption{Rule-derived feature weights. \texttt{ram} (3.00), \texttt{three\_g} (2.76), and \texttt{fc} (2.31) receive the highest weights, indicating they appear most frequently in high-lift class-specific rules.\label{fig:feature-weights}}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

All variants are evaluated using the same protocol:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item 5 random seeds: \{0, 10, 42, 100, 999\}
    \item \textbf{Hungarian algorithm} (\texttt{scipy.optimize.linear\_sum\_assignment}) maps cluster IDs to true labels optimally before computing accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 (all macro-averaged).
    \item \textbf{Clustering-native metrics}: ARI (Adjusted Rand Index) and NMI (Normalized Mutual Information) are also reported, which do not require Hungarian mapping.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analysis and Recommended Method}

The ablation study reveals a clear winner: \textbf{weighting-only is the recommended enhanced method.}

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Final comparison: Baseline K-Means vs.\ Recommended Method (Weighting-only)\label{tab:final-comparison}}
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
\toprule
Method & Accuracy & Precision & Recall & F1 & ARI & NMI \\
\midrule
Baseline K-Means & 0.2962 & 0.2959 & 0.2962 & 0.2941 & 0.0058 & 0.0078 \\
Weighting-only (recommended) & \textbf{0.4421} & \textbf{0.4250} & \textbf{0.4421} & \textbf{0.4285} & \textbf{0.1769} & \textbf{0.2109} \\
\midrule
Improvement & +14.6\,pp & +12.9\,pp & +14.6\,pp & +13.4\,pp & +0.171 & +0.203 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Feature weighting is the dominant component.} The weighting-only variant achieves the best accuracy (44.2\%) and ARI (0.177), outperforming the full method (38.9\%, ARI=0.101) and baseline (29.6\%, ARI=0.006). The key discriminative features --- \texttt{ram} (weight=3.0), \texttt{three\_g} (2.76), and \texttt{fc} (2.31) --- are upweighted by the rule-derived importance scores, providing a +14.6 pp accuracy improvement over baseline with no additional feature dimensions. This approach is best understood as a semi-supervised enhancement of K-Means: the labels guide the rule-mining step (feature engineering), while the clustering step itself remains purely unsupervised.
    \item \textbf{Pattern features alone hurt performance (negative finding).} The pattern-only variant (26.2\%) performs \textit{worse} than baseline (29.6\%), with ARI near zero. Appending 20 binary pattern features to the 20 original features introduces noise that dilutes the continuous feature signal. This is a valuable negative finding: \textbf{more features is not always better}, even when those features encode class-related information.
    \item \textbf{The full method improves over baseline but is not the best variant.} Combining weighting and patterns (38.9\%) substantially improves over baseline (29.6\%), yet falls short of weighting-only (44.2\%) on accuracy, recall, F1, ARI, and NMI. The pattern features partially counteract the benefit of feature weighting. This confirms that the pattern feature component should be discarded in the final method.
    \item \textbf{Clustering-native metrics confirm the ranking.} ARI and NMI follow the same ordering as classification metrics (weighting-only $>$ full $>$ baseline $>$ pattern-only), providing evidence that the cluster \textit{structure itself} improves, not just the post-hoc label mapping. The per-seed plots (Figure~\ref{fig:per-seed}) show this ranking is consistent across all 5 random seeds.
    \item \textbf{Limitations.} Even the best variant (44.2\%) remains far below supervised SVM (97\%). K-Means cannot capture non-linear decision boundaries regardless of feature engineering. The semi-supervised nature of this approach (using labels for rule mining) is also a limitation for purely unsupervised settings.
\end{itemize}
